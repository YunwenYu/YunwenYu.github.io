Project Overview
This project imagines a contemporary reissue of the Arecibo Message—not as a nostalgic replica, but as a speculative redesign. First transmitted in 1974, the original message was a binary-encoded radio signal sent into space, carrying simplified information about humanity and Earth.

Today, this project revisits that moment using current technologies, asking a simple but unsettling question: if we sent the message again now, what would we change—and why?
A reconstructed signal interface, where past transmissions meet contemporary data systems.
Looking Back
What Was the Arecibo Message
The original Arecibo Message was a carefully designed composition. It included numbers, chemical formulas, a DNA helix, a human figure, and a diagram of the solar system—all arranged in a grid of binary code. It assumed that mathematics could function as a universal language.

As astronomer Carl Sagan described, the goal was to create “a message that could be understood by any technological civilization.” It was optimistic, precise, and deeply shaped by the scientific worldview of its time.

But it was also selective. It presented a version of Earth that was clean, unified, and strangely silent about its complexities.
The message no longer travels alone—it exists within a network of satellites, archives, and code.

Retro Futures, Replayed
Reissuing the Arecibo Message today inevitably brings in retrofuturism—a reflection on how the past imagined the future. The original message now feels like a relic of an era that believed in clarity, progress, and universal solutions.

In this new project, retrofuturism becomes a visual and conceptual strategy. The pixel grids, binary structures, and monochrome diagrams remain—but they are layered with contemporary data aesthetics: glitch effects, satellite imagery, fragmented interfaces.
As Marshall McLuhan famously said, “the medium is the message.” Updating the medium—from radio signal to networked data stream—inevitably transforms the message itself.
The presence of the cassette tape introduces a different kind of technology—one tied to memory rather than progress. In contrast to high-speed data transmission, it suggests slowness, recording, and preservation. Within this project, the tape becomes a metaphor: what does it mean to archive Earth, rather than simply broadcast it? Retrofuturism here is not about going backward, but about layering temporalities. The message is no longer just sent—it is remembered.
New Technologies, New Earth
Data visualisation becomes language, reshaping how Earth is encoded and understood.
If the 1974 message was built on radio astronomy, the 2026 version would draw from a much broader technological ecosystem:

-Satellite imaging
-Climate data models
-AI-generated visualizations
-Global communication networks

Instead of a static binary grid, the new message might be dynamic, layered, even interactive.

It could include time-based data—showing Earth not as a fixed object, but as a changing system.
Earth is no longer seen as stable and self-contained, but as fragile, interconnected, and constantly in flux.
What Changed—and Why
The differences between the original and the reissued message reveal deeper cultural shifts.
Then:
-Confidence in science as universal truth
-Desire for simplicity and clarity
-A unified image of humanity
Now:
-Awareness of uncertainty and complexity
-Inclusion of multiple perspectives
-Recognition of ecological crisis
Even as we digitize Earth, we remain emotionally attached to it. The new message, therefore, cannot just be informational. It must also be affective—carrying traces of care, loss, and urgency.
Holographic projections replace static diagrams, turning information into a living system.
“We still have a deep yearning to see and smell the blooms of home.”
What This Project Does Well
One of the strengths of this reissued project is its ability to expand representation.
The original Arecibo Message reduced Earth to a set of universal constants.
The new version can include:
-Environmental data
-Cultural diversity
-Temporal change
It moves from a static portrait to a living archive.

It also aligns with contemporary design practices such as speculative design, where the goal is not just to communicate, but to provoke thought. The message becomes less about aliens and more about us—how we choose to represent ourselves.
The spacecraft drifts through space carrying fragments of Earth’s identity, but these fragments are no longer unified. Data, images, and signals overlap, sometimes contradicting one another. The retro-futuristic design softens this complexity, wrapping it in familiar visual language. It invites us in, even as it reveals uncertainty.
What It Overlooks
Inside the station, technology feels both advanced and strangely familiar. Buttons, dials, and analog textures coexist with holographic projections and algorithmic interfaces. This blending of eras reflects the core idea of the project: that the future is always built on imagined pasts. The Arecibo Message becomes a reference point, a starting signal that continues to echo. What changes is not just the technology, but the way we choose to represent ourselves.
However, this project is not without its blind spots.

In trying to include everything—data, diversity, complexity—it risks becoming overwhelming. The clarity of the original message is lost. What was once readable becomes dense and ambiguous.

There is also a bias toward technological representation. Even now, the message is shaped by those who control data and infrastructure. Entire ways of knowing—indigenous knowledge, non-digital cultures—may still be excluded.

As Gayatri Chakravorty Spivak warns, “The globe is on our computers. No one lives there.” The more we abstract Earth, the more we risk losing the lived realities it contains.
Overthinking the Audience
Another interesting tension lies in the intended audience. The original message was designed for extraterrestrials—but also for humans. It was a performance of intelligence and unity.
The reissued version may overcomplicate this idea. By focusing too much on accuracy and inclusivity, it forgets the speculative nature of the project. After all, we still don’t know who—or what—might receive the message.
In trying to say everything,
we may end up saying nothing clearly.
Why This Project Matters
Despite its limitations, this reissued Arecibo project is meaningful. It turns a historical artifact into a tool for reflection.

It asks:
How do we see ourselves today?
What do we choose to communicate?
What kind of future are we imagining?
In this sense, the project is less about space and more about Earth. It uses the idea of extraterrestrial communication as a mirror.
The holographic Earth replaces the flat, binary diagram of the original Arecibo Message. It rotates, updates, and responds—no longer a fixed image, but a dynamic system. This reflects a shift in how we understand the planet: not as a stable object, but as something in constant flux. The retro-futuristic interface frames this change, blending past optimism with present uncertainty. It is both a tribute and a quiet critique.
Conclusion:
A Message to Ourselves
Reissuing the Arecibo Message is not just an act of design—it is an act of reinterpretation. It reveals how much has changed since 1974, and how much remains unresolved.

The original message was a confident signal sent into the dark. The new version is more uncertain, more layered, and perhaps more honest.

And maybe that’s the point.
Every time we try to describe Earth, we are really describing who we think we are.
